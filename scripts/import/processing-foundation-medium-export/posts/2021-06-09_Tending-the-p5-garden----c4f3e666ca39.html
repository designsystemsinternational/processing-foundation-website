Tending the p5.garden 🌸

by Qianqian Ye

Growing up in Wenzhou, a coastal city surrounded by mountains in Southeast China, I spent a good amount of time in my grandparents’ garden. There were patches of bok choy, sweet potatoes, and scallions planted in soda cans when my grandmother didn’t have extra planters. The neighbors often came to hang out in the garden, bringing produce, like bittermelon, that they’d just picked from their yard as gifts. This inspired a childhood dream of mine, of building a community gathering space where people can meet, chat, and maybe exchange produce.

I often think that everything is like a garden: it takes time and care. My last name is Ye (叶 yè, pronounce like “yeah”), which means “leaf”🌱. I was born in mid-spring and my favorite color is green (#b1fc03). I always knew that I wanted to create spaces that are gentle and warm, without really knowing what that meant. I studied architecture in college and came to the US in 2012 as a graduate student in landscape architecture.

Architect Louis I. Kahn once said: “Architecture appears for the first time when the sunlight hits a wall.” But for me, architecture doesn’t appear until humans enter. During my graduate studies, I realized that I am more interested in creating space between people rather than the physical space itself, so I took a gap year in 2013 to work at architecture firms in Copenhagen. I did not know how I could create the gathering space my younger self had imagined but I wanted to try.

I was first introduced to p5.js after I left my job in an architecture firm in 2016, when my partner showed me how to make a running robot animation. Before that, my coding experience was minimal Visual Basic. I started to watch Coding Train tutorials and did daily-ish coding exercises on my own, and this first experience with p5 had a lot to do with “planting” green squares on GitHub. My main goal was to make daily commits so I could have green squares on the GitHub interface, which felt to me like planting vegetable patches in a digital garden. I never would have thought that I would end up making two sketches for the p5js.org homepage, one in support for Diversity in Art + Code in 2017, and another for the launch of the p5.js Chinese translation in 2018. As a non-binary person of color, resident alien, and someone who has had a hard time finding words for their identity, in this community I felt seen, welcomed, and included.

Teaching p5.js has formed most of my understanding about this project. In 2019, I made a series of p5.js video tutorials in Mandarin called Qtv as a Processing Foundation Fellow. The project started with the idea of teaching my mom, who lives in China and doesn’t speak English, how to code with p5. I wanted to make the coding tutorials feel intimate and grounded, so I recorded most of them in my backyard, with birds chirping, leaves rustling, and an avocado tree in the background. I was interested in exploring non-western narratives while teaching coding, so I used examples like Chinese calligraphy brush and urban villages in Shenzhen when I explained what random() is. I currently teach creative coding at USC Media Arts + Practice, where I introduce the students to coding through p5. I’ve learned a lot about how accessible and inclusive p5 is from my students. I have seen how it makes them feel less scared about programming, and how quickly it empowers students from all over just as it did for me. It makes them feel seen, welcomed, and included.

Evelyn and I met briefly (we both saw each other but were too shy to say hi) at Processing Community Day in January 2019; at that time we both didn’t know that we would be in the same cohort of the Processing Foundation Fellowship later that year. We became close friends after I moved to LA and had regular meetings to “talk about feelings.” We co-taught a three-weekend p5.js workshop at FCCW that centered data and gender, and in August 2019, we both participated in the p5.js Contributor’s Conference at CMU. One of my fondest memories from the p5.js Contributor’s Conference was that 30 of us, technologists, educators, artists, visited the botanical garden on the campus. I petted some of the plants and took a nap in the greenhouse. The experience in that conference felt soft, filled with care, like how I always feel about p5.

Together with Evelyn we applied and accepted the p5 lead role. We have similar values, balanced skills, and shared interests (like good poetry and bad memes). More importantly, we can “talk about feelings.” The p5 role is exciting and empowering, but also uncertain, scary, and overwhelming. I can’t imagine that I can lead this alone, or lead it with someone that I can’t be vulnerable with.

Leading p5 is like tending a garden, similar to my grandparents’ vegetable garden and the botanical garden at CMU. The p5 garden is the warm gathering space my younger self imagined, where people are welcomed to meet, chat, learn, and exchange ideas. I’d love to invite more gardeners to join the p5.garden, no matter how much gardening knowledge they have right now. Bring your seeds, let’s plant together. If you don’t have a planter, bring whatever you might have, like the used soda cans？
